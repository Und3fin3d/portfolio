Multiplayer chess on Flask and WebSockets

My friends and I played quite a lot of chess online, and the platforms we were using are built for millions of strangers rather than for about six people who all know each other already. Most of the things we actually wanted were either behind a subscription or lost somewhere in features meant for a global user base, so I ended up writing our own. It started as my A-level coursework and I have rebuilt it since.

Before writing any of it I surveyed the people who were going to use the thing, and compared the ten most popular platforms feature by feature against what they had asked for. That was mostly useful for telling me what to leave out. I dropped game chat and public forums, which sound like obvious features to include but I do not think they are: a group of friends is already talking on WhatsApp or a call whilst they play, chat on the bigger platforms gets abusive often enough that everybody ends up having to build word filters for it, and a forum is not worth much until you have a large number of users, which I was never going to have.

The rules are the hard part

Chess is more awkward to implement than people assume. Six pieces moving six different ways is tedious rather than difficult, and the trouble is really the exceptions.

Castling is the only move where two pieces move at the same time, and it is only legal if neither the king nor that particular rook has moved yet, the squares between them are empty, and the king is not in check and does not pass through a square that is under attack. The rook is allowed to pass through an attacked square, which I did not know before writing this. En passant needs the board to remember something: when a pawn goes forward two squares I set a flag on the square it skipped over, and that flag has to be cleared one move later, because the capture is only available immediately and not after that. Promotion is awkward in a different way, since it swaps the piece object out from under you in the middle of a move.

The engine itself is a Board holding Square objects, with the pieces as a class hierarchy so that each one answers the same legalmoves call in its own way. Working out legality happens in two stages: generate the pseudo-legal moves for a piece, then for each of those deep copy the whole board, play the move on the copy, and throw the move away if it turns out to leave my own king in check. It is the obvious way to do it and it is correct, but it is also easily the slowest thing in the program, which I will come back to.

The chess module only imports two things from the standard library, deepcopy for the check test above and split for reading FEN strings, so positions can be loaded from notation.

Two players, one board, no refreshing

A game is two browsers that need to agree on one board, so the server has to push updates instead of waiting to be asked for them. Flask does the routing and Flask-SocketIO carries the moves over WebSockets.

When a client connects and is signed in, it joins a room named after its own user id. That is one line of code and it is what makes direct challenges work, because to invite somebody the server can just emit into the room named after them and it will reach them wherever they are on the site. Pairing works much the same way, where a player emits a request with a time control and an increment on it, and the server either matches them with somebody already waiting on exactly those settings or opens a new board and leaves them in a waiting room until someone turns up.

Sessions use Flask-Login with remember me turned on, which matters more than it sounds like it would, because closing a tab by accident halfway through a game should not lose you the game.

Storing it

The database is MySQL through SQLAlchemy. A Player holds a username, an email, a hashed password, a rating and counts of wins, losses and draws. Passwords go through werkzeug's generate_password_hash so the database never actually stores one.

Friendship was the more interesting table to work out. It is many to many but from players to players, so it needs a self-referential association table where both sides join back to the same primary key. I set the relationship to load lazily as well, so fetching a player does not drag their whole friend list along with them.

I wrote tests for the rules engine rather than for the framework, on the basis that the framework is not the part I wrote. There are eighteen of them across the square and board objects, covering FEN loading, check detection, promotion, pseudo-legal and legal move generation and en passant capture, and they run in 0.169 seconds, which is quick enough that I actually ran them.

What the rebuild added

The second version has bughouse in it, which was the feature I wanted most and had listed as optional the first time round. It is four players across two boards, and any piece you capture gets handed to your partner, who can then drop it onto their own board instead of moving. For a group where people are at fairly different strengths it is the best format I know, because the weaker pair can still win it on the other board.

It turned out to be a reasonably neat inheritance problem too. The bughouse board subclasses the normal one and adds a pool of held pieces, with the FEN string extended to carry that pool, so everything that was already true about legal moves stays true and the only genuinely new thing is the drop. Friend lists, direct challenges, rematches and clock timeouts came in around the same time.

What I would change

The deep copy, mainly. Copying an entire board for every pseudo-legal move just to find out whether the king ends up in check allocates a whole position in order to answer a yes or no question. Two years later I wrote a chess engine on bitboards with make and unmake, where testing a move is a matter of flipping a few bits and then flipping them back, and that engine gets through hundreds of thousands of positions a second whereas this one copies the board to look at one of them.

In fairness it never mattered here, since a human takes several seconds over a move and the server only ever looks at the position in front of it. But it is the clearest example I have of solving the same problem twice, badly the first time, and understanding why the second attempt is faster is probably most of what I got out of writing both.
